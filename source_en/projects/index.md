<h2 class="gallery-title not-markdown" id="gallery-lenny-compass-title"><span data-i18n="gallery-lenny-compass-title">Lenny Compass</span></h2>
<p class="gallery-description" id="gallery-lenny-compass-description"><span class="gallery-preview" hidden></span><span class="gallery-full" data-i18n="gallery-lenny-compass-desc">Product knowledge is scattered across articles and podcasts, making it difficult to recover the original evidence behind a decision. I built a local workspace around Lenny’s newsletter and podcast archive, bringing questions, search, source reading and citation checks into one interface. Structured documents feed both keyword and vector indexes. Quick mode handles focused questions, while deep mode lets an agent search, read and select evidence over several rounds. An independent verification step checks each claim against its cited passages; unsupported claims are repaired or omitted. The application also provides persistent conversations, mode switching and targeted clarification questions. <h2 class="gallery-title not-markdown" id="gallery-analysisposts-title"><span data-i18n="gallery-analysisposts-title">AnalysisPosts</span></h2>
<p class="gallery-description" id="gallery-analysisposts-description"><span class="gallery-preview" hidden></span><span class="gallery-full" data-i18n="gallery-analysisposts-desc">Public-opinion research needs both statistical patterns in large collections of posts and reliable context about current events. A single agent can reach one-sided conclusions when its evidence is too narrow. I built a multi-agent workflow spanning data preparation, collaborative analysis and report generation. A data agent works with labeled records and quantitative tools, a search agent supplies external facts, and an explicit coordinator manages evidence gathering, cross-examination and conflict detection. During report generation, core claims are tied to sources and supporting evidence, then revisited by a verification agent. A visual interface exposes task steps, tool inputs and outputs, and scheduling paths. This makes it possible to inspect how conclusions emerged and locate execution problems, rather than relying only on the final report.</span> <button class="gallery-toggle" type="button" aria-expanded="true" aria-controls="gallery-analysisposts-description" hidden><span class="gallery-more" data-i18n="gallery-more">More</span><span class="gallery-less" data-i18n="gallery-less">Less</span></button></p>

<h2 class="gallery-title not-markdown" id="gallery-novel-eval-title"><span data-i18n="gallery-novel-eval-title">Novel Evaluation</span></h2>
<p class="gallery-description" id="gallery-novel-eval-description"><span class="gallery-preview" hidden></span><span class="gallery-full" data-i18n="gallery-novel-eval-desc">Fiction assessment often relies on editorial experience, genre intuition and market judgment. These standards can be difficult to explain or reuse consistently across different works. I built a local LLM-based evaluation system that turns such judgments into explicit rubrics. The workflow screens input, selects an analysis mode, identifies the work’s type, scores eight rubric axes and adds type-specific assessments. It then checks consistency and aggregates the findings into a structured report. The implementation includes task creation, result review, persistent history, prompt management and output-schema validation, alongside batch evaluation and regression tools. The aim is to let readers inspect the criteria and individual judgments behind an assessment, then refine the evaluation process instead of receiving only an opaque overall score.</span> <button class="gallery-toggle" type="button" aria-expanded="true" aria-controls="gallery-novel-eval-description" hidden><span class="gallery-more" data-i18n="gallery-more">More</span><span class="gallery-less" data-i18n="gallery-less">Less</span></button></p>

<h2 class="gallery-title not-markdown" id="gallery-mental-health-title"><span data-i18n="gallery-mental-health-title">Wearable Mental Health</span></h2>
<p class="gallery-description" id="gallery-mental-health-description"><span class="gallery-preview" hidden></span><span class="gallery-full" data-i18n="gallery-mental-health-desc">Periodic self-report questionnaires make it difficult to observe changes in mental health continuously. This research explored relationships between wearable physiological and behavioral measurements and mental-health status. We selected 43 variables from four years of observational data to construct a dataset of roughly 30,000 records at multiple time scales. Missing-value imputation, outlier handling and transformations of skewed variables prepared the data for modeling. Linear regression, random forests and gradient boosting supplied base models, which were combined through an ensemble approach and tuned using grid search. The result was a modeling workflow from wearable measurements to predictions of mental-health status. The project received support from the National College Student Innovation and Entrepreneurship Training Program and concluded in May 2024.</span> <button class="gallery-toggle" type="button" aria-expanded="true" aria-controls="gallery-mental-health-description" hidden><span class="gallery-more" data-i18n="gallery-more">More</span><span class="gallery-less" data-i18n="gallery-less">Less</span></button></p>

<h2 class="gallery-title not-markdown" id="gallery-health-metrics-title"><span data-i18n="gallery-health-metrics-title">Public Health Metrics</span></h2>
<p class="gallery-description" id="gallery-health-metrics-description"><span class="gallery-preview" hidden></span><span class="gallery-full" data-i18n="gallery-health-metrics-desc">A single health indicator cannot capture the many dimensions of population health or explain how different factors relate to modernization. For this statistical-modeling project, I combined health indicators from the National Bureau of Statistics and commercial databases, alongside collected trends in medical research topics. Principal component analysis organized the correlated indicators, and two components were selected for their interpretable meanings. Sparse regression then helped identify relevant factors, with grid search balancing model fit against the number of retained variables. The final model achieved an R-squared of approximately 0.89, and the project received a regional first prize in the National College Student Statistical Modeling Competition. The emphasis was on turning scattered data into health measures that could be compared, interpreted and investigated further.</span> <button class="gallery-toggle" type="button" aria-expanded="true" aria-controls="gallery-health-metrics-description" hidden><span class="gallery-more" data-i18n="gallery-more">More</span><span class="gallery-less" data-i18n="gallery-less">Less</span></button></p>
