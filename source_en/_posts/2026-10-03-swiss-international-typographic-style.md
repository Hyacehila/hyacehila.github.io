I have seen similar designs on many websites and in PowerPoint presentations: clear type, left-aligned text, a photograph, and generous whitespace. I like this style and want to understand how it works, so I can use it more confidently in future websites and presentations.

Swiss Style is also known as the International Typographic Style. It developed in Switzerland during the 1950s, with Zurich and Basel serving as important centers of design and teaching. It drew on modernist traditions such as the Bauhaus. Josef Müller-Brockmann and Armin Hofmann were among its leading figures. Their concern was how to organize type and images so that information could be communicated clearly. [MoMA's The Modern Poster](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1804_300074240.pdf)

A useful starting point is the **grid system**. Designers establish margins, column widths, and spacing in advance, then arrange headings, body text, and images along shared lines. The grid is usually invisible in the finished work, but it determines the order of the page. It also allows asymmetric compositions: a narrow text block on one side and a large image on the other can still feel balanced through alignment and proportion.

Type and alignment are distinctive, too. Sans-serif typefaces such as Helvetica and Univers are classic choices, often used with asymmetric layouts. [SWI on Swiss typography](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/culture/switzerland-still-a-font-of-creativity/41628070) In practice, I can begin with one type family and distinguish headings, body text, and captions through size, weight, and spacing. Source Han Sans is an option for Chinese text. Left-aligned body text with a ragged right edge gives every line a consistent starting point and avoids stretching character spacing merely to make the edges line up.

Whitespace separates different pieces of information and helps important content stand out. Photography can directly show a person, object, or scene, with cropping establishing a relationship to the text. Whitespace paired with a clean photograph makes a useful beginner's exercise; traditional Swiss design also uses photomontage, so photographs need not be presented exactly as they were taken. [House of Switzerland on Swiss Style](https://houseofswitzerland.org/swiss-stories-archives/swiss-new-style)

To apply these ideas to PowerPoint, you can start with a ready-made slide master: standardize the fonts, title position, and page numbers; set up text and image placeholders in the layouts; and use guides to establish margins and alignment. A six-column grid is one possible starting point, followed by three basic layouts: a cover, text with a photograph, and a chart. The presentation can then remain consistent as the content changes.

The same approach can work for a website: establish the content width, columns, and spacing before adding text and images. On a phone, change multiple columns into a single column and reconsider the reading order, while keeping the text at a comfortable size.

In practice, choose tools according to the format you want to deliver. For an editable PPTX, you can try [PPT Master](https://github.com/hugohe3/ppt-master), which offers Swiss minimalism and other visual styles. For a browser-based presentation, [guizang-ppt-skill](https://github.com/op7418/guizang-ppt-skill) includes Swiss Internationalist layouts and generates HTML slides. For websites, you can pair AI-assisted frontend development with the “Minimalism & Swiss Style” guidance in [UI/UX Pro Max](https://github.com/nextlevelbuilder/ui-ux-pro-max-skill) to help choose type, colors, and layouts. If you prefer a visual builder, [Webflow](https://webflow.com/grid) lets you set grids and spacing and adjust layouts for different screen sizes.

After getting a first draft, check alignment, whitespace, and photo cropping so that the output follows the design rules you established.
